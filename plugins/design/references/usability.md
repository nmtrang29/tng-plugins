# Usability Fundamentals

## What — Definition of Usability

Usability is a **quality attribute** that assesses how easy user interfaces are to use. The word "usability" also refers to methods for improving ease-of-use during the design process.

Usability is defined by **5 quality components**:

- **Learnability** — How easy is it for users to accomplish basic tasks the first time they encounter the design?
- **Efficiency** — Once users have learned the design, how quickly can they perform tasks?
- **Memorability** — When users return to the design after a period of not using it, how easily can they reestablish proficiency?
- **Errors** — How many errors do users make, how severe are these errors, and how easily can they recover from the errors?
- **Satisfaction** — How pleasant is it to use the design?

Beyond these, another key quality attribute is **utility**, which addresses functionality: does the design actually do what users need it to do?

Usability and utility carry equal weight, and together they determine whether something is genuinely useful. Ease-of-use means little if the system doesn't offer what you want, and a system with the right features is wasted if the interface is too hard to operate. The same user research methods used to study usability can also be applied to evaluating utility.

- **Utility** = whether it provides the **features you need**
- **Usability** = how **easy & pleasant** these features are to use
- **Useful** = **usability + utility**

## Why Usability Matters

On the web, usability is essential to survival. Difficult sites lose visitors. Homepages that fail to convey what a company offers lose visitors. Users who get lost, encounter unreadable content, or can't find answers to their questions all leave. The pattern is consistent: people don't read manuals or struggle to decode interfaces when countless alternative sites are a click away. Abandoning the site is the default response to friction.

> The first law of ecommerce is that if users cannot _find_ the product, they cannot _buy_ it either.

For **intranets and internal tools**, usability translates directly into employee productivity. Every minute employees spend lost or puzzling over instructions is paid time that produces no work.

Current best practices suggest devoting roughly **10% of a design project's budget** to usability. On average, doing so more than doubles a website's desired quality metrics and almost doubles intranet quality metrics. Software and physical products see smaller but still meaningful gains when usability is prioritized.

For internal projects, picture doubled usability as halved training costs and twice as many transactions completed per hour. For customer-facing designs, picture twice the sales, twice the registrations or leads, or twice the value of whichever KPI drove the project.

## How to Improve Usability

Of the many methods available for studying usability, the most fundamental and valuable is **user testing**, which has 3 components:

- Get hold of some **representative users** — customers for an ecommerce site, employees for an intranet (and in the intranet case, ideally from outside the project's own department).
- Ask the users to perform **representative tasks** with the design.
- **Observe** what the users do, where they succeed, and where they have difficulties with the user interface. Stay quiet and let users do the talking.

Test users one at a time and allow them to work through problems independently. Helping them or pointing out specific screen areas contaminates the results.

For uncovering a design's most significant usability problems, testing about 5 users is generally sufficient. Instead of pouring resources into one large study, run a series of small tests and revise the design between rounds, fixing issues as they surface. **Iterative design is the surest path to a better user experience** — the more interface variations you put in front of users, the better the outcome.

User testing differs from focus groups, which serve poorly as a tool for evaluating design usability. While focus groups have value in market research, judging interaction designs requires closely watching individuals work through tasks with the interface. What people say is unreliable; what they do is what counts.

## When to Work on Usability

Usability plays a role at every stage of the design process. Because multiple studies are needed, keeping each one fast and inexpensive is essential. The main steps:

1. Before beginning a new design, **test the old design** to identify which parts work well and should be preserved or highlighted, and which parts cause user trouble.
2. Unless you're building an intranet, **test competitors' designs** for inexpensive insight into alternative interfaces tackling similar features.
3. Conduct a **field study** to observe users in their natural environment.
4. Build **paper prototypes** of one or more design concepts and test them. Invest as little time as possible in these early ideas — they'll all need revision after testing.
5. Refine the most promising concepts through **multiple iterations**, progressing gradually from low-fidelity sketches to high-fidelity working prototypes. Test at every stage.
6. Evaluate the design against **established usability guidelines**, drawing on either your own prior research or published findings.
7. After settling on and implementing the **final design**, test it once more. Subtle usability issues invariably appear during implementation.

Avoid postponing user testing until a design is fully built. By then, most critical problems uncovered by testing will be impossible to fix — many are structural and would demand significant rearchitecting.

The only route to a genuinely high-quality user experience is to begin testing early and continue testing throughout the process.

## Where to Test

If you conduct at least one user study per week, investing in a dedicated usability laboratory makes sense. For most organizations, however, a conference room or office works fine — provided the door can shut out distractions. The essentials are real users and your presence beside them as they interact with the design. The only equipment strictly required is a notepad.
