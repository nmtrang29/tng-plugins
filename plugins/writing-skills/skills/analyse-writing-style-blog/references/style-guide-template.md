# Style Guide Template

The canonical structure for a writing-style analysis. Follow this format unless the source material demands a clear deviation.

## File header

Start with:

```
# <Subject>'s Writing Style and Tone Guide

Captured from analyzing <N> of their <source type> (e.g. "Google Docs at Datadog" / "blog posts at example.com"). Sample drew from <date range> and spanned <list of genres / doc types>.
```

If the subject's preferred spelling of their name differs from the filename, add a one-line note.

## Required sections

### 1. Core Voice

One paragraph. The reader should be able to recognise the subject after reading this paragraph alone. Pack it with concrete observations:
- Register (formal/casual/conversational/declarative)
- Default stance toward the reader (peer/authority/coach/diagnostician)
- What's distinctive (compared to a generic AI-writing register)
- What the voice trusts to do the persuasive work (evidence/structure/metaphor/charisma)

### 2. Voice and Perspective

Three to five sub-sections, each with bullet observations and 1–3 quoted examples. Cover at minimum:
- First-person vs. second-person vs. third-person register
- Hedge style (heavy hedging? confident declarations? what specific hedge words recur?)
- Confidence vs. vulnerability balance — when do they admit limits?
- Spelling conventions (British/American/mixed)
- Casual register markers (contractions, abbreviations, lowercase)

### 3. Narrative Structure

One paragraph naming the overall arc(s), then a bulleted list of **Common Section Patterns** by genre:
- Strategic / analytical docs: ...
- Design reviews / spec docs: ...
- Peer feedback: ...
- Self-eval / promo: ...
- Personal / reflective: ...

Each pattern should be a concrete recipe (intro → middle → end shape), not a vague description.

### 4. Structural Patterns

Several sub-sections:
- **Heading Conventions** — sentence case vs Title Case? full sentences vs noun phrases? question-shaped? bracketed type tags?
- **Punctuation and Visual Style** — em-dash usage, colon usage, parenthetical asides, italics, bold, emoji, ALL CAPS, date stamps, status pills
- **Opening Hooks** — patterns by genre, with examples
- **Section Organisation** — depth of nesting, table usage, list-vs-prose ratio
- **Transitions** — recurring transition phrases or lack thereof
- **Closing Style** — soft close, action close, restate close, trail-off close? what's their default?

### 5. Tone Characteristics

Sub-sections covering:
- Diagnostic vs declarative
- Empathetic vs decisive
- Self-aware vs self-promotional
- Humour style (where it appears, how often, what register)
- Handling disagreement
- Vulnerability and admission of limits

Each with quoted examples.

### 6. Tone Shifts

How the register changes across genres / contexts. Aim for one line per identified genre: "In X, the voice does Y."

### 7. Content Themes

What they tend to write about. Group thematically. Specific to this subject — don't pad with generic categories.

### 8. Language Choices

Sub-sections:
- **Signature Expressions and Moves** — recurring phrases, idioms, rhetorical pivots, with examples
- **Metaphors and Analogies** — what they reach for; do they extend metaphors or use them briefly; named metaphors and where they appear
- **Emphasis Techniques** — bold vs italics vs ALL CAPS; how punctuation does emphasis
- **Technical / Business Writing** — domain vocabulary used; whether jargon is grounded or assumed

## Required appendices

### Authenticity Markers

Bulleted list of specifics that make the voice traceable to this person:
- Names dropped (colleagues, projects, conferences)
- Specific dates / quarters / years
- Recurring tools, products, brands
- Internal vocabulary (org-specific terms)
- Numeric specifics (revenue, MAU, etc.) if observed

### What to Avoid

Three sub-lists — patterns the subject AVOIDS:
- **Tone** — buzzwords, registers, hedging styles
- **Structure** — opening patterns, closing patterns, length conventions
- **Style** — punctuation, emoji, ALL CAPS, false confidence, etc.

### Practical Application

A 12–20 step cheat sheet titled "When writing in <Subject>'s voice:". Each step is a concrete instruction, not abstract advice. Match the voice's actual moves.

## What makes a good guide

- **Specificity over generality.** "Uses hedges" is weak. "Frequent hedges: *'a bit'*, *'a little'*, *'kinda'*, *'imo'*, *'ish'*. Hedges almost always precede a firm claim — softens delivery, not substance." is strong.
- **Quoted examples.** Every observation should land near a short verbatim quote.
- **Negative space.** Note what's absent as evidence — no exclamation marks, no emoji except ⌛, no thesis-first openings — these are as informative as what's present.
- **Cross-genre consistency.** Note what stays the same across registers — that's the voice. Note what shifts — that's the register dial.
- **No hallucinated patterns.** If you can't find an example, drop the observation. Don't pad with plausible-sounding generic AI-writing observations.

## Anti-patterns in the analysis itself

- Don't summarise content. The reader should not learn what the subject *writes about* from this guide — only how they write.
- Don't write "they use a friendly tone" — name the friendliness markers. *"Polite imperatives, 'Feel free to...', occasional :), British spelling"* is what makes a tone friendly in this specific way.
- Don't list every observation — group and cluster. Five tight sub-sections beat thirty floating bullets.
- Don't pad with AI-style boilerplate. If a section is short because the subject is consistent and quiet, that's fine.
